    <tr><th colspan="3">1. General Design Principles</th></tr>
    <tr>
      <td>Mobile Design</td>
      <td>Show the most important information and actions immediately. Hide less-used features under additional navigation to reduce scrolling and clutter.</td>
      <td>Bottom navigation for main sections, expandable menus for secondary options, sticky buttons for important actions.</td>
    </tr>
    <tr>
      <td>Aesthetic-Usability Effect</td>
      <td>People often perceive attractive interfaces as easier to use, even when they are not more functional. A pleasing appearance creates a positive emotional response and increases perceived usability.</td>
      <td>Consistent colors and fonts, balanced spacing, attractive buttons with clear labels.</td>
    </tr>
    <tr>
      <td>Occam's Razor</td>
      <td>When several solutions work equally well, prefer the simplest one with the fewest unnecessary assumptions. Remove elements that do not support the user's goal without compromising functionality.</td>
      <td>Fewer checkout steps, simpler navigation, forms asking only for necessary information.</td>
    </tr>
    <tr>
      <td>Pareto Principle</td>
      <td>Roughly 80% of results often come from 20% of causes. Focus effort on the features and improvements that provide the greatest value to users.</td>
      <td>Prioritize popular features, improve common user journeys, fix frequent usability problems first.</td>
    </tr>

    <tr><th colspan="3">2. Cognitive Psychology and Decision-Making</th></tr>
    <tr>
      <td>Cognitive Bias</td>
      <td>A systematic error in thinking or judgment that influences how people perceive situations and make decisions. Mental shortcuts based on past experiences help people decide quickly but can also lead to incorrect judgments without their awareness.</td>
      <td>Familiar brands influencing trust, default options guiding choices, reviews influencing purchases.</td>
    </tr>
    <tr>
      <td>Cognitive Load</td>
      <td>The mental effort required to understand and use an interface. Intrinsic load comes from the task itself, while extraneous load comes from unnecessary or distracting design elements that do not help users understand the content.</td>
      <td>Clear labels, simple layouts, advanced features revealed only when needed.</td>
    </tr>
    <tr>
      <td>Hick's Law</td>
      <td>The time needed to make a decision increases with the number and complexity of choices. Too many options lead to choice overload, making decisions harder and reducing satisfaction. Break complex tasks into smaller steps, highlight recommended options, and make comparisons easier. Do not simplify so much that important choices disappear.</td>
      <td>Simplified menus, a few recommended plans, filters to narrow results, progressive onboarding.</td>
    </tr>
    <tr>
      <td>Miller's Law</td>
      <td>Working memory temporarily holds the information needed to complete a task. It is often described as holding around seven items, plus or minus two, but capacity varies and information fades quickly. Do not use seven as a strict limit. Break information into meaningful chunks and help users recognize information instead of remembering it.</td>
      <td>Grouped navigation links, split phone numbers, multi-step forms, preserved form details, visible breadcrumbs.</td>
    </tr>

    <tr><th colspan="3">3. Gestalt Principles and Visual Perception</th></tr>
    <tr>
      <td>Law of Proximity</td>
      <td>Elements placed close together are perceived as related, while elements farther apart are perceived as less related. Spacing helps users understand relationships and organize information.</td>
      <td>Labels beside input fields, related buttons grouped together, larger gaps between sections.</td>
    </tr>
    <tr>
      <td>Law of Similarity</td>
      <td>Elements that look alike are perceived as belonging to the same group, even when separated. Color, shape, size, orientation and movement can signal that elements share a purpose.</td>
      <td>Matching button styles, consistent icons, links visually different from ordinary text.</td>
    </tr>
    <tr>
      <td>Law of Continuity</td>
      <td>The eye tends to follow smooth paths and aligned elements, perceiving them as connected. Arrange content to guide attention naturally through an interface.</td>
      <td>Aligned form fields, timelines, visual paths guiding users through steps.</td>
    </tr>
    <tr>
      <td>Common Region and Connectedness</td>
      <td>Elements inside the same clearly defined area, or connected by lines, borders or colors, are perceived as more related than unconnected elements.</td>
      <td>Product cards containing images and prices, bordered form sections, lines connecting process steps.</td>
    </tr>
    <tr>
      <td>Law of Prägnanz</td>
      <td>People interpret complex or ambiguous visuals in the simplest way possible because simple patterns require less mental effort. The brain also fills in missing parts of incomplete shapes (closure) when enough information is available to recognize the whole.</td>
      <td>Clear icons, uncluttered pages, partially outlined shapes, minimalist logos.</td>
    </tr>

    <tr><th colspan="3">4. Memory, Attention and Perception</th></tr>
    <tr>
      <td>Serial Position Effect</td>
      <td>People tend to remember the first and last items in a series better than those in the middle. Place important information and actions where they are more likely to be noticed and remembered.</td>
      <td>The most important menu items placed at the start or end of the list, the main action button placed at the end of a form, key details shown at the top of a page.</td>
    </tr>
    <tr>
      <td>Von Restorff Effect</td>
      <td>Also called the Isolation Effect. When several similar elements appear together, the one that differs is more likely to be remembered. Use emphasis sparingly and do not rely on color alone to communicate meaning.</td>
      <td>Distinctive primary buttons, highlighted recommended plans, visually distinct warnings.</td>
    </tr>
    <tr>
      <td>Selective Attention</td>
      <td>Users focus on information relevant to their goals and often ignore distractions. Banner blindness occurs when users ignore content that resembles advertising. Change blindness occurs when users fail to notice interface changes because their attention is elsewhere.</td>
      <td>Clear visual hierarchy, separate ads from navigation, noticeable cues for important changes.</td>
    </tr>
    <tr>
      <td>Peak-End Rule</td>
      <td>People judge an experience largely by how they felt at its most intense moment and at its end, rather than by every moment equally. Negative experiences may be remembered more vividly, so focus on important moments and a positive ending.</td>
      <td>Helpful error recovery, clear purchase confirmations, smooth checkout completion.</td>
    </tr>

    <tr><th colspan="3">5. Mental Models and User Expectations</th></tr>
    <tr>
      <td>Jakob's Law</td>
      <td>Users spend most of their time on other products, so their mental model (what they believe about how a system works) comes from those experiences. Match these expectations with established patterns so users can focus on their tasks instead of learning new interactions.</td>
      <td>Shopping cart icons, familiar search bars, conventional checkout steps.</td>
    </tr>
    <tr>
      <td>Paradox of the Active User</td>
      <td>Users often start using software immediately instead of reading instructions, even if learning first could save time later. Make guidance available throughout the experience and show it where it is needed.</td>
      <td>Tooltips for unfamiliar controls, tips beside advanced settings, contextual error guidance.</td>
    </tr>

    <tr><th colspan="3">6. Interaction, Performance and Complexity</th></tr>
    <tr>
      <td>Fitts's Law</td>
      <td>The time needed to select a target depends on its size and distance. Larger targets are easier to select, while small targets increase the chance of mistakes. Make important touch targets large, well-spaced and easy to reach.</td>
      <td>Large mobile buttons, spaced navigation icons, frequently used controls near the thumb.</td>
    </tr>
    <tr>
      <td>Doherty Threshold</td>
      <td>Productivity improves when systems respond quickly, ideally within 400 milliseconds. Fast feedback helps users maintain attention. Animations and progress bars make longer waits more tolerable. A deliberate pause can sometimes make an important result feel more trustworthy.</td>
      <td>Immediate button feedback, loading animations, upload progress bars.</td>
    </tr>
    <tr>
      <td>Postel's Law</td>
      <td>Be liberal in what you accept and conservative in what you send. Accept reasonable variations in user input while providing clear, consistent output. Anticipate different inputs and explain errors helpfully.</td>
      <td>Phone numbers with or without spaces, flexible date formats, clear validation messages.</td>
    </tr>
    <tr>
      <td>Tesler's Law</td>
      <td>Also called the Law of Conservation of Complexity. Every system has unavoidable complexity that must be handled by either the system or the user. Designers should manage as much complexity as possible rather than passing it to users. Larry Tesler argued that engineers should spend an extra week simplifying applications instead of making millions of users spend extra minute dealing with unnecessary complexity. Bruce Tognazzini observed that people may attempt more complex tasks when a system becomes simpler.</td>
      <td>Automatic calculations, sensible defaults, guided setup that handles technical choices.</td>
    </tr>

    <tr><th colspan="3">7. Motivation and Task Completion</th></tr>
    <tr>
      <td>Flow</td>
      <td>A state of deep focus, involvement and enjoyment that occurs when a task matches the user's skill level. Tasks that are too difficult cause frustration, while tasks that are too easy cause boredom. Provide feedback, remove unnecessary friction and make features easy to discover.</td>
      <td>Simple task sequences, immediate action feedback, gradually introduced advanced features.</td>
    </tr>
    <tr>
      <td>Goal-Gradient Effect</td>
      <td>People tend to become more motivated as they get closer to completing a goal. Showing progress and what remains can encourage users to finish a task.</td>
      <td>Onboarding progress bars, completed-step checklists, remaining-step indicators.</td>
    </tr>
    <tr>
      <td>Zeigarnik Effect</td>
      <td>People tend to remember unfinished or interrupted tasks better than completed ones. Show incomplete work and make it easy to continue, without creating unnecessary pressure.</td>
      <td>Saved drafts, incomplete setup checklists, multi-step progress indicators.</td>
    </tr>
    <tr>
      <td>Parkinson's Law</td>
      <td>People tend to use all the time they are given for a task, even if it could be done faster. Reducing the actual time needed to complete a task can improve the user experience. Remove unnecessary steps and automate repetitive work.</td>
      <td>Autofill personal details, saved payment information, short booking forms.</td>
    </tr>
  </tbody>
</table>
